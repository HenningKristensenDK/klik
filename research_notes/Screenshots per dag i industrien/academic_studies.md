# Academic and peer-reviewed studies: how often people take screenshots and copy-paste at work

Scope note: compiled 2026-11-22. Primary PDFs were read for Woodruff & Alexander 2019, Stolee et al. 2009, Mottelson 2023 and Hu & Lee 2022. Other figures come from abstracts, search snippets or secondary citations and are labelled as such. Main result up front: no peer-reviewed study found reports a measured, natural, per-day screenshot rate for desktop knowledge workers. Clipboard use is well measured (desktop: about 3 to 5 operations per active hour, more for programmers).

## HCI studies on screenshot behaviour: what rates were measured (screenshots per day/week)?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study found measures how many screenshots people take per day of their own accord, on desktop or at work. The closest measured figures are a stored-library count on iPhones (mean 498.8 screenshots stored, median 195; consumer, mostly US, 2023) and a prompted desktop field study (16.3 screenshots per person per week, but participants were told to take "a few each day"). Most "screenshot studies" in HCI capture the screen automatically every 5 seconds as a research tool, so their counts say nothing about user behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- **Mottelson 2023, "Why do people take Screenshots on their Smartphones?", DIS '23 (ACM), IT University of Copenhagen.** Mobile (iPhone only). Crowdsourced study, 52 participants recruited through social media, aged 20 to 48, from seven countries, 68% US. Participants shared 1,679 screenshots with free-text reasons (1 to 100 each). Occupations not reported; a general consumer sample, not workers. — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3563657.3596067)
  - Mean number of screenshots stored in the photo library: 498.8 (SD 705.3, median 195). This is a stock, not a rate, and the paper gives no capture period, so no per-day figure can be derived from it. — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
  - About 24% of screenshots were taken to share with others; 76% were for personal use or unintended. Activity peaks at 8 pm, lowest at midnight, with local dips around 5 am and noon (from metadata of 21,535 screenshots). — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
  - Most common words in the reasons: funny, friend, remember, share, reference, picture, boyfriend, love. Nine themes overall; "Jobs" appears only as a sub-theme. — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
  - Mottelson states there are "remarkably few empirical investigations of why people take and store screenshots". — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
- **Cramer, Sang & Park 2019, "Uses and gratifications of the screenshot in human communication: an exploratory study".** Survey of a mostly college-age student sample about devices, frequency and motives. The abstract says screenshots "occur often and on the go" but gives no per-day figure. As cited by Mottelson: 97% of respondents had sent a screenshot to someone else. Sample size and journal not confirmed. — [University of Canberra record](https://researchprofiles.canberra.edu.au/en/publications/uses-and-gratifications-of-the-screenshot-in-human-communication-/); [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
- **Jaynes (cited in Mottelson 2023).** Ethnography with 27 teenagers on the social role of screenshots. Qualitative; no rates. — [Mottelson 2023 PDF](http://aske.mottelson.dk/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Mottelson2023b.pdf)
- **Hu & Lee 2022, "Scrapbook: Screenshot-Based Bookmarks for Effective Digital Resource Curation across Applications", UIST '22, Virginia Tech.** Desktop (macOS). Closest to knowledge work. — [arXiv 2209.12318](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.12318)
  - Field study: 13 participants (mostly graduate students, two undergraduates, one software engineer, one data analyst; mean age 26.4), one week. They were told to "take a few screenshots each day" with the tool. Result: 212 screenshots in total, an average of 16.3 per user per week (SD 6.2, min 6, max 32), roughly 2.3 per day. **Prompted, not natural behaviour.** — [arXiv 2209.12318](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.12318)
  - Need-finding survey: N=125 people who use computers for work (social media, Amazon Mechanical Turk, campus mailing lists), mean age 32.6, reported 8.6 hours per day at a computer. The survey asked about bookmarks and task resumption, not screenshot frequency. — [arXiv 2209.12318](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.12318)
- **Karlson et al. 2010, "Mobile Taskflow in Context: A Screenshot Study of Smartphone Usage", CHI 2010, Microsoft Research.** Participants took screenshots on their phones as a diary method and annotated them later. Screenshots are the instrument, not the behaviour studied. Per-day counts not seen in what was retrieved. — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/p2009-karlson.pdf)
- **Automatic-capture studies (not user behaviour).** Yang, Ram, Robinson & Reeves, CHI EA 2019: capture every 5 seconds, 3,055 hours of smartphone use, 2,199,654 screenshots. Guo et al., CHI 2025: about 34K screenshots from 17 Android users over one week. Screenomics framework: 5-second intervals. These counts measure the logging rate, not how often people press a screenshot key. — [Yang et al. 2019, ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290607.3313089); [Guo et al., arXiv 2410.11354](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.11354); [Screenomics PDF](https://jenpan.com/jen_pan/screenomics.pdf)
- **Messaging privacy study.** Semi-structured interviews with 20 members of a private Facebook group: most used screenshots to bookmark or keep information needed in everyday life. Qualitative; no rates. — [Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication 30(1), zmae023](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/30/1/zmae023/7978203)

### Inferences
- "Remember" and "reference" rank high among reasons, next to social sharing. That supports screenshots as personal information management, but on phones and in private life.
- 76% of phone screenshots are for personal use, not sharing. If that holds on desktop (unverified), the capture-to-own-notes flow may matter as much as the capture-to-chat flow.
- The Scrapbook figure (about 2.3 per day) is a floor for a prompted tool trial in a student-heavy group. It must not be quoted as a natural rate for professionals.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that logs natural, user-initiated desktop screenshots (PrintScreen, Win+Shift+S, Snipping Tool, Snagit, Greenshot) per person per day or week.
- No study found that reports screenshot frequency for professionals at work, by role or industry, or in Denmark/the Nordics.
- The Cramer et al. 2019 frequency categories and sample size were not retrieved (full text not accessed).
- Mottelson's 498.8 stored screenshots could be turned into a rate only with the age of each library, which the paper does not give.

## Clipboard and copy-paste studies: measured frequency, image vs text, cross-application

### Takeaway
Desktop clipboard use is measured in three peer-reviewed field studies: 3 to 4 operations per user per hour for end users (US, 2009), 5.3 per active hour for a mixed, tech-heavy UK group over 90 days (2015 data, published 2019), and about 16 copy-paste instances per hour for professional programmers (2004). About 30% of transfers cross application boundaries. On phones, copying is rare (0.69 items per person per day). None of these studies measured image clipboard content; the loggers recorded only text and files.

### Cited Findings
- **Woodruff & Alexander 2019, "Data Transfer: A Longitudinal Analysis of Clipboard and Drag-and-Drop Use in Desktop Applications", International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, Lancaster University (doi 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2019.08.005).** Desktop, Windows 7/8.1/10. — [Authors' accepted manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581919301028)
  - Method: 90-day logging study with the MultiLog plug-in logger (keyboard and mouse hooks plus Windows UI Automation), on participants' own computers, starting September 2015. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Sample: 17 participants, UK, aged 18 to 74, 5 female. Occupations: 5 PhD students (3 CS, 2 design), 3 CS lecturers, 3 CS undergraduates, and one each of freelance blogger, software engineer, researcher, personal assistant, IT systems manager and retired. 5 used the computer for work only, 3 for home only, the rest for both. The authors note that many had technical backgrounds. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Volume: 31,567 clipboard operations: 12,677 copy (40.1%), 2,680 cut (8.4%), 16,210 paste (51.3%). — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Rate: 5.3 clipboard operations per "interaction hour" (any hour with user input), SD 4.2. 8 participants were under 5 per hour, 5 were at 5 to 10, and 4 were over 10. 1.6 unused cut/copy operations per hour (about 30% of cut/copy). Drag-and-drop: 0.6 per interaction hour. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Cross-application: 69.5% of clipboard operations stayed within the same application, so about 30% crossed applications. 74.4% of between-window transfers needed only one window switch; the mean was 1.6 switches. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Content type: 28,939 (92%) text and 2,628 (8%) files. The logger recorded only text length or file count; "information about other types of objects is typically unavailable". **Image clipboard content was not measured.** — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Timing: mean 13.7 s between copy/cut and paste; 76.7% under 10 s. 70% of operations started from keyboard shortcuts. 61% of text transfers within documents were under 50 characters. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - Most frequent applications for data transfer: IDEs, presentation editors, spreadsheets, email clients, text editors. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
  - The paper does not report total interaction hours or a per-day rate. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
- **Stolee, Elbaum & Rothermel 2009, "Revealing the Copy and Paste Habits of End Users", IEEE VL/HCC 2009, pp. 59-66, University of Nebraska-Lincoln.** Desktop. — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf); [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5295296/)
  - Sample: 25 downloaded the monitor and 15 completed. Group 1: 7 administrative assistants and teachers. Group 2: 8 non-CS-major students in an intro CS course. Four weeks of regular activity; average observation over 50 hours per user. — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf)
  - Volume: 2,544 clipboard interactions: 1,158 copies (45%), 71 cuts (3%), 1,315 pastes (52%). — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf)
  - Rate: "between three and four" clipboard interactions per hour per user. The highest user averaged nearly 20 per hour. — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf)
  - Applications (share of all clipboard interactions): word processors 26%, web browsers 23%, email clients 19%, spreadsheets 18%, IDEs 5%, file system 4%. About 81% of copied data was pasted. Users did nearly 80% of clipboard work in two applications and 97% within five. — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf)
  - Within vs between applications: 70% within, 30% between, as summarised by Woodruff & Alexander. — [Woodruff & Alexander manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
- **Kim, Bergman, Lau & Notkin 2004, "An Ethnographic Study of Copy and Paste Programming Practices in OOPL", ISESE 2004 (IBM T.J. Watson and University of Washington, doi 10.1109/ISESE.2004.10).** Professional programmers, mainly Java. Observation plus an editor logger. — [IBM Research record](https://research.ibm.com/publications/an-ethnographic-study-of-copy-and-paste-programming-practices-in-oopl); [IBM tech report RC23217](https://dominoweb.draco.res.ibm.com/reports/rc23217.pdf)
  - Rate: about 16 copy-paste instances per hour, cited in both Stolee 2009 and Woodruff & Alexander 2019. The authors' slides give a mean of about 16 per hour and a median of about 12. — [Stolee 2009 PDF](https://kstolee.github.io/assets/papers/stolee_VLHCC2009.pdf); [Kim ISESE'04 slides](https://web.cs.ucla.edu/~miryung/Publications/isese04-copypaste-slides.pdf)
  - Sample: Woodruff & Alexander describe five programmers over about 50 hours; a search summary of the IBM report says about 60 hours of observed coding. The exact figure is unconfirmed. 74% of copy-paste actions were a single line such as a variable, type or method name. — [Woodruff & Alexander manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf); [IBM tech report RC23217](https://dominoweb.draco.res.ibm.com/reports/rc23217.pdf)
- **Li & Myers 2021 (data from February-March 2016), "A Need-finding Study for Understanding Text Entry in Smartphone App Usage", arXiv preprint, Carnegie Mellon.** Mobile (Android). 17 participants recruited at CMU, mostly students, aged 18 to 45 (mean 25.2). Two weeks of logging through an accessibility service plus a diary. — [arXiv 2105.10127](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.10127)
  - 164 unique items copied: 0.69 per participant per day. 158 paste events (about 0.66 per participant per day, derived). — [arXiv 2105.10127](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.10127)
  - 2.7% of entered characters were pasted (Section 5.1). The introduction says copy-paste was "less than 1%" of text entry; the paper is internally inconsistent on this. Not peer-reviewed as far as found. — [arXiv 2105.10127](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.10127)
- **Clinical documentation (different unit).** An ECRI report cites residents using copy/paste 0.8 times per note in one setting. Unit is per note, not per day; source not read in full. — [ECRI copy/paste report](https://www.ecri.org/resources/hit/htais_copy_paste_report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rough per-day translation, assuming 6 to 8 active computer hours (Hu & Lee's survey reports 8.6 self-reported hours): about 20 to 30 clipboard operations per day for general end users (Stolee) and about 30 to 40 for Woodruff & Alexander's group. These are my estimates, not study results.
- Paste is about half of all clipboard operations in both desktop studies, so a capture tool that ends with the image already on the clipboard fits the dominant action.
- About 30% of transfers are cross-application, and that share was stable from 2009 to 2015. A screenshot is almost always a cross-application transfer (screen to chat, mail, document), which is the less common, more effortful case.

### Gaps
- No academic study found that measures the share of image (bitmap) clipboard content vs text. Both desktop loggers ignored image formats.
- No published academic analysis found of Windows clipboard history (Win+V) or Snipping Tool telemetry.
- Microsoft's "paste is among the most used commands in Office" data exists in blog and talk form, not as a peer-reviewed study; not verified here.
- No post-2015 desktop clipboard logging study found.

## Software engineering studies: screenshots in bug reports, issues, Stack Overflow, code review

### Takeaway
Software engineering studies measure the share of artifacts that contain images, not how often developers take screenshots. On GitHub, 9% to 25% of issues contain images depending on the sample; in Android bug reports about 16% contain images, and almost all of those are UI screenshots. No per-developer screenshot rate was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Kuramoto et al. 2022, "Do Visual Issue Reports Help Developers Fix Bugs? A Preliminary Study of Using Videos and Images to Report Issues on GitHub", ICPC 2022 (ERA track).** 226,286 issues from 4,173 public GitHub repositories: 18,760 (9.09%) contain images and 1,230 (0.54%) contain videos. Issues with images contain fewer words; resolution time is not significantly different. — [Kuramoto ICPC 2022 preprint](https://mkmknd.github.io/assets/preprint/kuramoto2022ICPC.pdf); [ICPC 2022 programme](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icpc-2022/icpc-2022-era/9/Do-Visual-Issue-Reports-Help-Developers-Fix-Bugs-A-Preliminary-Study-of-Using-Vide)
- **Kuramoto et al. 2024, "Understanding the characteristics and the role of visual issue reports", Empirical Software Engineering.** 683,810 issue reports from 34 active GitHub repositories. A search snippet gives 17.10% containing images and the number 79,212. Those two figures do not reconcile (79,212 / 683,810 = 11.6%), so neither is confirmed. — [Kuramoto EMSE 2024 PDF](https://posl.ait.kyushu-u.ac.jp/~kamei/publications/Kuramoto_EMSE2024.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380173683_Understanding_the_characteristics_and_the_role_of_visual_issue_reports)
- **Jupyter Notebook case study (arXiv 2204.13262, 2022).** 1,071 of 4,210 issues (25.4%) contained at least one visual element. In 781 of those (72.9%), the visual was in the original issue post. — [arXiv 2204.13262](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.13262)
- **Android bug reports (search snippet, attributed to the ImageR / image-in-bug-report literature, 2025).** 7,988 of 50,988 Android bug reports (15.67%) include images, and 95.18% of those images are UI screenshots. Exact paper attribution not confirmed. — [ImageR, arXiv 2505.01925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01925); [Wang et al., MSR 2025, arXiv 2502.15099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15099)
- **Wang, Zhang, Feng, Halfond & Yu 2025, "An Empirical Study on Leveraging Images in Automated Bug Report Reproduction", MSR 2025.** Studies UI screenshots as an image type in bug reports; dataset figures not retrieved. — [arXiv 2502.15099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15099)
- ImageR cites prior work saying 22.5% of images in issue reports do not help resolve the issue (secondary; not checked). — [ImageR, arXiv 2505.01925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01925)

### Inferences
- Roughly one issue in ten on general GitHub, and one in four or six in UI-heavy projects, carries an image. Screenshots are a routine but minority part of written developer communication.
- These are artifact shares, not people rates; they cannot be turned into screenshots per developer per day.

### Gaps
- No study found that reports screenshots per developer per day or week, or screenshot use in code review.
- Stack Overflow image prevalence studies exist (for example, work on images in Stack Overflow questions), but figures were not retrieved within the search budget.
- The Android 15.67% / 95.18% figures need confirming against the source paper.

## Engineering, construction and project contexts: screenshots in BIM/CAD coordination, RFIs, issue tracking

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study found measures how often screenshots or markups are used in construction, BIM/CAD coordination, RFIs or project issue tracking. The literature describes coordination issues and meeting tools; screenshots appear only as a practice that BCF (BIM Collaboration Format) is meant to replace.

### Cited Findings
- **Mehrbod et al. 2019, ITcon (Journal of Information Technology in Construction).** Ethnographic study of design coordination on MEP-heavy projects (University of British Columbia). The most frequent coordination issues were design errors, discrepancies and missing items; temporal and functional issues took longest to resolve. Teams switched often between 2D and 3D artifacts in meetings. No screenshot counts. — [Mehrbod et al. 2019, ITcon](https://www.itcon.org/papers/2019_03-Mehrbod.pdf)
- **BCF practice (vendor source, not academic).** BCF files attach comments and snapshots (screenshots with a saved viewpoint) to the IFC model as a structured alternative to sending screenshots with text. — [BIMcollab, "The challenges of good issue management"](https://www.bimcollab.com/en/?p=643)
- An MDPI Buildings paper is reported in a search summary as calling reliance on screenshots and text descriptions inefficient compared with structured viewpoints. Opinion, not measurement; the passage was not read directly. — [MDPI Buildings 7(3):75](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-5309/7/3/75)

### Inferences
- The fact that BCF builds snapshots into each issue suggests that annotated images are the default unit of coordination communication. That is an inference from tool design, not a measured frequency.

### Gaps
- No measured rate found for screenshots or markups per engineer, per RFI or per coordination issue.
- Not checked: whether BCF issue datasets (for example from BIMcollab, Solibri or ACC/BIM 360) have been analysed academically for snapshots per issue. That would be a possible proxy.

## Desktop activity logging studies (time-tracking, RescueTime-like logs) that report screenshot or Snipping Tool events

### Takeaway
No academic desktop activity log study found reports user-initiated screenshot or Snipping Tool events. Desktop logging studies either take screenshots themselves at fixed intervals (ScreenTrack, Screenomics) or log clipboard and window events without screenshot keys (Woodruff & Alexander).

### Cited Findings
- **ScreenTrack (arXiv 2001.10898).** Records a visual history of the screen at 5-second intervals to help users retrieve documents and web pages. Automatic capture; not a user screenshot rate. — [arXiv 2001.10898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10898)
- **Screenomics.** Fixed 5-second capture of the screen as a research framework. — [Screenomics PDF](https://jenpan.com/jen_pan/screenomics.pdf)
- **Woodruff & Alexander 2019.** Their MultiLog dataset also included window switches and other mouse and keyboard-shortcut logging, but the paper reports no PrintScreen or Win+Shift+S figures. — [manuscript](https://www.jasonalexander.kiwi/pdf/IJHCS_DataTransfer.pdf)
- **Commercial time trackers (not academic).** Tools such as Hubstaff take automatic screenshots (up to 3 per monitor per 10-minute interval while tracking). This is a vendor setting, not evidence of user behaviour. — [Hubstaff support](https://support.hubstaff.com/accessing-thumbnail-gallery-activity-page/)

### Inferences
- The missing measure (user-initiated desktop screenshots per day) is a real gap in published research, not just a gap in this search. Several authors call screenshot behaviour "poorly understood" (Mottelson 2023).
- Klik could produce this number with its own opt-in, local event counter, but that would need consent and would have to respect the README's local-by-default promise.

### Gaps
- No academic paper found with Snipping Tool, Snip & Sketch, Snagit or PrintScreen event frequencies.
- Industry telemetry (Microsoft, TechSmith) and vendor surveys were out of scope for this note; another researcher may cover them.
